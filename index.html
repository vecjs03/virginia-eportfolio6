Alongside the lab, I worked where science meets people with a difficult hand dealt to them: therapeutic recreation at Parkwood Mental Health Institute, and now the Fowler Kennedy sport medicine clinic.

I find passion through organizing initiatives so other people can take part.

Care that continues even when treatment ends

Chemotherapy can push someone into menopause decades early. Survivorship care is it's own challenge for navigating what happens next.

The wicked problem

A growing population of cancer survivors, assigned female at birth, live with chemotherapy-induced premature menopause — and survivorship programs generalize the long-term physical and psychosocial challenges they face.

Our guiding question

How can care programs be redesigned to meet the long-term needs of women affected by chemotherapy-induced premature menopause — across the entire treatment journey?

01 · MSC JUNE 2026

Communicating Science in the 21st Century

Six formats, four weeks, one question underneath: who is this for?

The course overview

This course explored how scientific ideas can be communicated across different formats and audiences. Through written, oral, and creative assignments, I practiced making complex research more accessible without losing accuracy or meaning. This course spanned LinkedIn posts, a microteach, a lay summary, a grant proposal, a podcast, and a debate on open source scientific literature.

During this course, I was able to adapt science literature across different formats for different audience, navigate a peer review process from both sides as the applicant and reviewer, and evaluate newer forms of communication such as pre-prints and social media.

The lesson: communicating science isn't simplifying it. It's choosing your audience, purpose and platform deliberately, then holding accuracy steady while everything else is carefully coordinated to communicate effectively.

WHERE MY THINKING WAS CHALLENGED

In the debate, I wanted to argue for open access. I was assigned the opposition — and had to sit with misinformation, misinterpretation, and findings travelling without their context. Access alone isn't understanding. Switching perspectives is a useful skill that is difficult to master.  could you truly argue for a side you disagree with?

FEATURED ASSIGNMENT

Lay Summary on using fNIRS and tennis to find awareness after severe brain injury

A layman's summary written as a mock media release · group work

We rewrote a 2025 study from Dr. Karnig Kazazian's team at London Health Sciences Centre for a general reader. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy — a bedside tool, no transport needed — looked for brain activity in ICU patients asked to imagine playing tennis.

25% of 32 patients showed brain activity on command
50% of those responders were later discharged from the ICU, able to follow commands

Our opening line is what taught me the assignment: roughly 70% of deaths after severe brain injury follow a decision to withdraw life support. Burying that in paragraph six would have been accurate and useless.

It stood out because I was writing about someone's family in the worst week of their life. Falsely hopeful or clinically cold would both have been failures of the science, not just the prose for a news article.
Lay Summary on Detecting Awareness in the Intensive Care Unit Using Functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy
Kazazian et al. (2025)
PRESENTATION

The Microteach: The Three Disciplines of Fencing

A three-minute live presentation · individual
Three disciplines of Fencing · Wyllie (2015)

Three minutes to teach a room something they knew nothing about. I chose fencing: where the sport came from, how to tell a foil from an épée from a sabre, what the uniform is doing, and how the strategy differs between the three weapons.

I opened with The Princess Bride — a reference nearly everyone in the room already knew — then used a real blade and whiteboard diagrams to separate the three disciplines.

What it taught me is that three minutes is a brutal editor. The challenge isn't knowing the material — it's deciding what earns a place, in what order, and what context a listener needs first in order for anything after it to land. Confidence carries the delivery; critical judgment decides the content.

"You are a natural presenter! I loved that your introduction tied your, somewhat niche, topic to something that most of the audience would be familiar with in order to pull us all in. You really controlled the whole space you were given, and maintained a good pace and a happy medium between a professional and conversational tone."

Peer review
What I took forward

The consistent notes were to move around less and watch the microphone. Since then I practice every aspect of a presentation prior to the execution, down to what clothes I will wear to avoid microphone feedback.

ASSIGNMENT

The Podcast: making a paper audible

AI and large language models in medicine · A ten-minute episode · groupwork

Ten minutes, four voices, one article — and an audience that cannot see a single figure. Every diagram we might have leaned on had to be rebuilt in speech.

I opened with a personal story tied to the topic, because a listener commits to a voice before they commit to a subject. We also deliberately played up contrast between the four of us, so listeners could tell instantly who was speaking and follow the argument by voice alone.

Podcast assignment · AFP (2025)

Audio strips away every visual crutch, which makes it the most honest test of whether you actually understand something. If you cannot describe a study design out loud in a sentence someone can hold in their head, you have not finished thinking about it.

"Wow, what a good discussion! This was a great podcast episode, focusing on a currently hot topic... You guys did a really good job setting up the context with a personal anecdote, and discussing the implications of this topic and the results of the paper. You really put the findings into a real world context."

Instructor feedback
What I took forward

Two notes stayed with me: name the paper, authors and date on air — the listener has no file to check — and push harder on underrepresented populations. Large language models learn from the data that exists, and medical research has historically failed to include diverse and female subjects, which means the gap gets inherited by the tool. That point deserved more than a mention, and it is the kind of omission I now look for first.
Skills built
  • Writing for a named audience.
  • Signposting and creating a story structure. 
  • Writing a peer review and the response letter. 
  • Arguing a position on its merits rather than my agreement with it.
Carried forward

Audience awareness is the transferable skill of the whole program. Researching who the target audience is, what they care about, how they communicate is the same tool for a policy brief, a lay summary, and a capstone targeted solution.

02 · MSC JULY 2026

Academic Integrity & Professionalism

I came in thinking integrity was a checklist. I left thinking of it as a practice, who's challenge arises most in everyday grey areas.

The course overview

Before this course, misconduct meant the obvious cases: plagiarism, fabrication, falsification. After this course I have come to learn of many more ethical questions that integrate themselves into the scientific community. We discussed further issues of power imbalances and AI disclosures which showed just how much damage can happen quietly instead.

During this course, I was able to apply academic integrity principles to various ethical scenarios, expand the definition of professionalism to multiple professional settings, and discern what distinguishes moral conduct vs potential areas of academic and professional misconduct.

Food for thought: Responsible AI use is usually declared at the very end of a process, at submission. Someone who used it badly has every incentive to describe it just inside the acceptable range. So how do we keep everyone accountable?

HOW MY THINKING WAS CHALLENGED

"Ethics is not just an abstract intellectual discipline. It is about the conflicts that arise in trying to meet real human needs and values."

John Ziman
FEATURED ASSIGNMENT

The Retraction Story Assignment

An ethical discussion about public retraction of academic literature · individual
Retraction Watch Article on: RETRACTED ARTICLE: The Antidiabetic Metformin as an Adjunct to Antidepressants in Patients with Major Depressive Disorder: A Proof-of-Concept, Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Trial
Abdullah et al. (2020)

I traced one retracted paper all the way down: a 2020 trial claiming metformin, added to fluoxetine, dramatically improved outcomes in major depressive disorder. Retracted in 2022 when the journal lost confidence in the results.

How it was caught

A psychiatrist noticed the adverse-event counts differed by exactly one patient for seventeen of eighteen side effects.

Fabrication was never proven. The failure was transparency and documentation — and it was catchable by one careful reader.

I paired it with a retraction that showed integrity: a JAMA Network Open paper on video gaming and children's cognition, retracted and replaced because the authors found their own analytical errors and documented every correction.

ASSIGNMENT

The Misconduct Scenario: personal relationships in the lab

A case scenario analysis of a supervisory conflict of interest · group work

Scenario Context
We were handed a scenario that can be all too familiar to many professional settings... a potential budding romance in the workplace but without concrete evidence.
A technician reports that a senior graduate student, who had been helping oversee an undergraduate thesis project, has been arriving, troubleshooting, and leaving with that undergraduate. The technician started to suspect a romance. Neither student has said anything. The next lab meeting is tomorrow, and the technician wants it addressed.

Misconduct Scenario #7 · personal relationships in the lab (July 2026)
The position I took

Speak to the students — privately and separately, never at the lab meeting. Working the same hours or going to lunch proves nothing, but a mentoring role over someone's thesis is a real power dynamic, and the concern deserved a conversation rather than an audience.

What I took forward

Manage the conflict, don't punish the people. I also proposed a concrete fix beyond the scenario: add a module to Western's onboarding training on how to navigate an emerging relationship and who to talk to, without framing it as a punishable offence — because a system that only threatens consequences teaches people to hide things. Ethics work looks less like enforcement and more like designing conditions where disclosure is the easy choice.

Is a relationship itself misconduct?
After researching various institutional policies, my conclusion was not necessarily — what matters is how it formed and who is affected. If it began after the thesis term started, the positional difference compromises true consent, and Western's conflict of interest policy names supervisor–student and intimate relationships as grounds for a reasonable apprehension of bias.
After educating myself on Western University's specific policy, my recommendation was to remove the graduate student from any evaluative role, document the steps taken, and give the whole lab a general reminder about boundaries without naming anyone.

My instinct at the start of this course was to look for the rule that had been broken. Here, nothing had been — and acting as though something had would have done the damage itself. Raising it publicly would have embarrassed two people, implied wrongdoing before anything was understood, and cost the trust that makes a lab function.

The harder realization was that a relationship makes communication more difficult on both sides: the undergraduate may not want to disappoint the person mentoring them, and the graduate student may not be able to give objective feedback. Protecting people meant redesigning the reporting line, not judging the relationship.
Skills built
  • Recognizing a conflict of interest before it becomes a complaint. 
  • Reading institutional policy and applying it to a live situation. 
  • Reasoning through ethical grey zones. 
  • Evaluating whether a published claim can be trusted.

Carried forward

Source evaluation. The next section on Science Policy runs on grey literature, where some sources aren't pre-vetted. Knowing how to approach a claim with a critical lens makes a better reader. Furthermore, using reasoning backed up with evidence-based claims allows for intentional solution proposals and consideration of those affected.

03 · MSC August 2026

Science Policy

Where I learned that a good idea and the implementable of the idea are their own respective challenges of innovation.

The course overview

This course pushed me to consider real life frameworks on a large scale close to home: Canada. Science policy is a bridge between healthcare and the government. My group and I had to come up with something to ameliorate patient involvement in drug development. We came up with an innovative idea that works alongside Canada's real health structure — Health Canada's mandate, Canadian Institutes of Health Research's Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (CIHR's SPOR), the Canadian Drug Agency — to propose something that could actually work within Canada's government framework.

That meant identifying a policy lever rather than a non-designated idea, and being specific about who does the work and what measures could indicate successful implementation.

During this course I developed an understanding in science policy within the federal government, learned various principles of different types of management (risk and change) and how to implement them, and navigate teamwork group skills to capitalize off of individual strengths.

WHERE MY THINKING WAS CHALLENGED

Within science academia, we are heavily encouraged to use peer-reviewed articles. Within Science Policy most of my sources weren't peer-reviewed, they were government pages, framework documents, and environmental scans — grey literature. Reading that material critically, asking whose perspective is present and whose is missing, is what the course actually taught me. The ability to critically evaluate each source and use creative thinking for tangible solutions is a careful skill to hone. 

FEATURED PRESENTATION

Canadian Patient Involvement in Medicines Traceability and Development (C-PIMTD) Framework

The Canadian Patient Involvement in Medicines Traceability and Development Framework.

A nation wide initiative to promote patient involvement in drug development · August 2026
Overview of our proposed solution to increase patient involvement in Canada's drug development framework (August 19, 2026)
Presented to Health Canada's Chief Regulatory Officer, David K. Lee

Canada is genuinely good at asking patients what they think — just later in the drug development process (at reimbursement, and when research funding is already decided). This poses an issue: once input is gathered, it's difficult to evaluate and measure if any meaningful change occurred or if patient input is contributing to an echo chamber due to limited influential power.

Rather than build a parallel system, we adapted the European Patients' Academy on Therapeutic Innovation (EUPATI) and Patient Focused Medicines Development (PFMD) to Canada's context across six stages of the drug development pathway.

Three moving parts
01 Traceability. Document what patients raised, what was adopted, and why any opinions weren't used.
02 Training. A modular Health Canada package, modelled on the current TCPS CORE-2022 ethics training.
03 Coordinators. A federal network building pathways into rural, remote and equity-deserving communities.

My contribution was the solution's concept and description — which was only one component of proposing a solution. Compressing six stages into something a regulator can hold in their head forced us to decide what the idea actually was and furthermore how to fit it into the complex Canadian network of levers, frameworks, and jurisdictional influence.
The moment I'm proudest of

We presented to the Chief Regulatory Officer of Health Canada and were commended for the creativity of the idea at a national scale. Standing in front of someone who does this for a living, and having the concept hold up was truly an inspiring experience.

PRESENTATION SLIDES ASSIGNMENT

Engagement Strategy: Patient Involvement in Drug Development

Stakeholder analysis and engagement plan · groupwork

A framework only works if the people it depends on will actually use it. This assignment asked us to map every stakeholder C-PIMTD affects — ten of them, from patients to pharmaceutical sponsors to the FDA — and design how we would engage each one.

We used a RACI (Responsible; Accountable; Consulted; Informed) framework to assign each stakeholder a level of engagement based on their influence and interest, then wrote a strategy, a goal, and measurable success criteria for every group, alongside the reaction we expected from them.

What I took forward

Success criteria are where an engagement plan becomes real. "Consult patients" is a sentence; "document what changed because of patient feedback" is something someone can be held to. I now write the measure at the same time as the intention — if I cannot say how anyone would know it happened, the proposal is not finished.

The exercise made the politics of implementation impossible to ignore. Forty percent of our stakeholders sit inside government, which makes coordination the real constraint — and influence never divided evenly by category. Provinces had to be approached as voluntary partners rather than subjects of a federal requirement, because framing alone could sink participation.

Writing the expected reactions was the most useful part. Anticipating that sponsors would worry about cost and timelines, and that patient organizations would worry about tokenism, changed what we proposed rather than just how we pitched it.
